A reductio doesn't prove its conclusion by building toward it; it proves
by ruling out the alternative until only one option can still be
standing. Mathematicians talk about an "elegant" proof the way a film
editor talks about a clean cut --- fewer moves, nothing carried that
isn't load-bearing --- and Occam's razor is the same instinct promoted to
a general principle: prefer the account that needs the fewest assumptions,
not because simpler is prettier, but because every extra assumption is
one more thing that could be wrong.

Paul Erdős's notion of "The Book" --- an imagined volume God keeps of the
most elegant proof of every theorem --- makes explicit what this course has
been circling since week 1: a discipline can have a shared, largely tacit
standard for which cut was the right one, argued about, sometimes
overturned, and taken completely seriously by people who'd never call it
taste.

## Outline

- reductio ad absurdum: proving by elimination rather than construction
- Occam's razor as elimination promoted to a general principle
- Erdős's Book, and mathematics's own version of "taste is what you
  leave out"
